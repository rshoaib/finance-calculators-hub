<p class="lead">Picture two identical 62-year-olds with identical work histories. One files for Social Security today and collects about $2,600 a month for life. The other waits eight more years, files at 70, and collects roughly $4,600 a month for life — that's 77% more, every month, forever, for the same earnings record. That spread is the single biggest financial lever most retirees control, and one of the most misunderstood. This guide walks through the 2026 rules, the break-even math, and the trade-offs nobody tells you about.</p>

<p>We'll use 2026 figures published by the Social Security Administration and assume a Full Retirement Age (FRA) of 67, which now applies to everyone born in 1960 or later.</p>

<h2>The three ages that matter</h2>

<p>Social Security lets you start benefits any month between age 62 and 70. Three ages anchor the decision:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Age 62 — earliest eligibility.</strong> You can file, but you take a permanent benefit cut.</li>
  <li><strong>Age 67 — Full Retirement Age (FRA).</strong> For anyone born in 1960 or later, this is when you receive 100% of your <a href="https://www.ssa.gov/benefits/retirement/planner/agereduction.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Primary Insurance Amount</a> (PIA) — the benefit calculated from your 35 highest-earning years.</li>
  <li><strong>Age 70 — maximum benefit.</strong> Every month you delay past FRA earns "Delayed Retirement Credits" (DRCs) up until age 70. After 70, there's no further increase, so filing later only throws money away.</li>
</ul>

<p>Filing before FRA triggers a permanent reduction. Filing after FRA triggers a permanent increase. Both adjustments last for the rest of your life — and typically for your surviving spouse's life too.</p>

<h2>How the reduction math works</h2>

<p>For someone with an FRA of 67, the reduction schedule follows a two-tier formula set by the SSA:</p>

<ul>
  <li>The first 36 months you claim early reduce your benefit by <strong>5/9 of 1% per month</strong> (6.67% per year).</li>
  <li>Each additional month beyond 36 reduces by <strong>5/12 of 1% per month</strong> (5% per year).</li>
</ul>

<p>Delayed Retirement Credits for anyone born in 1943 or later are <strong>two-thirds of 1% per month</strong> — a flat 8% per year — up to age 70. Stack those together and you get the table below.</p>

(That's close to the national median for a mid- to upper-middle-income career earner; the SSA reports the average retired-worker benefit in 2026 is about $2,071 after the 2.8% <a href="https://www.ssa.gov/news/en/cola/factsheets/2026.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">cost-of-living adjustment</a>.)</p>

<p>Two things jump out. First, the age-70 check is nearly double the age-62 check. Second, filing at 62 still produces the biggest <em>lifetime total</em> if you die at 85, because you collected for eight extra years. The tradeoff is real. That's why break-even analysis matters.</p>

<h2>The break-even age: when waiting pays off</h2>

<p>For each pair of claiming ages, there's a specific age at which total lifetime benefits cross over. Miss that age and the earlier claim "wins" in cumulative dollars; live past it and the later claim wins. The standard break-even ages, using the FRA = 67 schedule above and ignoring COLAs (which push break-even slightly earlier because the absolute dollar gap widens each year):</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>62 vs 67:</strong> roughly <strong>age 78–79</strong>. If you expect to live past mid-70s, waiting to 67 nets more total lifetime benefits.</li>
  <li><strong>67 vs 70:</strong> roughly <strong>age 82–83</strong>. Delaying from FRA to 70 comes out ahead if you live past your early 80s.</li>
  <li><strong>62 vs 70:</strong> roughly <strong>age 80–81</strong>. The full eight-year delay pays off if you make it into your 80s.</li>
</ul>

<p>Here's the uncomfortable part: a healthy 62-year-old today has a life expectancy into the mid-to-late 80s, per SSA's 2026 actuarial tables. A man reaching 65 is expected to live to about 84; a woman to about 87. Married couples almost always see at least one spouse past 88. In other words, most people who <em>can afford to wait</em> would collect more lifetime dollars by waiting — often substantially more.</p>

<h2>Why the max-earner numbers are worth knowing</h2>

<p>The SSA's published maximums for 2026 illustrate the delay premium in sharp relief. The maximum benefit for someone hitting FRA in 2026 is approximately <strong>$4,152 per month</strong>. The same maximum earner filing at age 70 in 2026 can receive up to approximately <strong>$5,181 per month</strong> — a difference of more than $12,000 per year, for life, indexed annually for inflation. (Sources vary slightly on the exact ceiling; the SSA fact sheet is the authoritative reference.)</p>

<p>Your number won't be that high unless you hit the <a href="https://www.ssa.gov/news/en/cola/factsheets/2026.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Social Security wage base</a> ($176,100 in 2026) for 35 or more years. But the same percentage multipliers apply to everyone. Whatever your PIA is, claiming at 70 gives you 124% of it; claiming at 62 gives you 70%.</p>

<h2>When claiming at 62 actually makes sense</h2>

<p>Filing early isn't always wrong. It can be the right call if:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>You need the income.</strong> If Social Security is the only thing standing between you and depleting your portfolio, eating the reduction may still beat liquidating investments at a bad time. Use a <a href="/retirement-calculator">retirement calculator</a> to model the portfolio impact side by side.</li>
  <li><strong>Your health is poor or family history is short.</strong> Break-even math assumes average longevity. If you have reason to expect a shorter life, early benefits capture more lifetime dollars.</li>
  <li><strong>You've stopped working and have no other income.</strong> Since the <a href="https://www.ssa.gov/benefits/retirement/planner/whileworking.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">earnings test</a> only applies if you're working, retirees with no wage income aren't giving anything up to take benefits early.</li>
  <li><strong>You want to let a portfolio keep growing.</strong> Some retirees prefer drawing Social Security at 62 and letting their 401(k) or IRA compound longer, especially if they're comfortable with market exposure.</li>
</ul>

<h2>When waiting to 67 — or 70 — is the smart move</h2>

<p>Delaying generally wins if any of these apply:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>You're in good health and your family lives long.</strong> Longevity is the single biggest predictor that delay pays off.</li>
  <li><strong>You're still working and your earnings are above the limit.</strong> In 2026, if you claim before FRA and earn more than $24,480 in wages, the SSA withholds $1 for every $2 earned above that limit. The withheld benefits are credited back later, but filing early while working is rarely optimal.</li>
  <li><strong>You're the higher earner in a married couple.</strong> Your benefit sets the floor for your spouse's <em>survivor benefit</em>. Waiting to 70 doesn't just raise your check — it raises the check your surviving spouse collects for the rest of their life.</li>
  <li><strong>You have other assets to bridge the gap.</strong> If you can cover living expenses from cash, a taxable brokerage, or a bridge from a 401(k) until 70, the 8% annual delayed retirement credit is effectively a risk-free inflation-adjusted return the market rarely matches.</li>
</ul>

<h2>The earnings test: a trap to watch for</h2>

<p>If you claim benefits before your FRA and keep working, the <strong>Social Security earnings test</strong> kicks in. For 2026:</p>

<ul>
  <li>If you're under FRA all year: SSA withholds <strong>$1 for every $2</strong> you earn above <strong>$24,480</strong>.</li>
  <li>In the year you reach FRA: SSA withholds <strong>$1 for every $3</strong> you earn above <strong>$65,160</strong>, counting only the months before you hit FRA.</li>
  <li>Starting the month you reach FRA: <strong>no earnings limit</strong>. You can earn unlimited income with no reduction.</li>
</ul>

<p>The withheld benefits aren't gone — at FRA, SSA recomputes your benefit to credit back the months that were fully offset. But it still often makes sense to just wait if you're planning to keep working.</p>

<h2>Married couples: the two-claim optimization</h2>

<p>If you're married, the "when to claim" question becomes two questions. A common optimization:</p>

<ol>
  <li>The <strong>lower-earning spouse</strong> claims earlier (sometimes at 62–65) to bring household cash flow in sooner.</li>
  <li>The <strong>higher-earning spouse</strong> waits as long as possible, ideally to 70, to maximize the monthly check <em>and</em> the eventual survivor benefit.</li>
</ol>

<p>When one spouse dies, the survivor keeps the larger of the two benefits. Maximizing the higher earner's benefit means maximizing the widow(er)'s income for the remainder of their life — which, given that women outlive men by about three years on average, often means 10+ years of substantially higher income for the survivor.</p>

<h2>Taxes don't disappear in retirement</h2>

<p>Up to 85% of Social Security benefits can be taxed at the federal level once your "combined income" exceeds certain thresholds (those thresholds are not indexed for inflation, so more retirees cross them each year). Thirteen states still tax Social Security to some degree, though the list has been shrinking. Before deciding on a claim age, model your benefits as part of total taxable income using our <a href="/tax-bracket-calculator">tax bracket calculator</a> and our <a href="/retirement-calculator">retirement calculator</a> so the net-of-tax numbers drive the decision, not the gross.</p>

<h2>How to decide: a simple framework</h2>

<p>Most financial planners boil the decision down to four questions:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Do you need the income now?</strong> If yes, the question is partly answered — take it. If no, keep going.</li>
  <li><strong>What's your realistic life expectancy?</strong> Healthy, non-smoker, long-lived parents → you're likely on the "delay pays off" side of the curve.</li>
  <li><strong>Are you still working?</strong> If yes and you're under FRA, the earnings test will claw back much of what you'd collect.</li>
  <li><strong>Do you have a spouse who will depend on your survivor benefit?</strong> If yes, maximizing your check by waiting is a form of life insurance for them.</li>
</ol>

<p>If the answers lean "don't need it, long life expectancy, still working, spouse needs the survivor benefit" — waiting to 70 is almost always the math-backed choice. If they lean the other way, claiming earlier is perfectly reasonable.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

<h3>Can I change my mind after I claim?</h3>

<p>Yes, within a short window. Within 12 months of your first benefit, you can file Form SSA-521 to withdraw your application — but you must repay every dollar you received. After 12 months, or after FRA, your only option is to suspend benefits (which stops checks and earns Delayed Retirement Credits until 70). You can't simply un-file years later.</p>

<h3>Does claiming at 62 reduce my spouse's survivor benefit?</h3>

<p>Partially. Your surviving spouse can step up to 100% of what your benefit <em>was</em> at your death if they're at their own FRA. If you filed at 62 and took a 30% reduction, that reduced benefit is the floor for the survivor benefit in most cases, which is a meaningful lifetime hit if your spouse is likely to outlive you.</p>

<h3>What if I'm divorced?</h3>

<p>If your marriage lasted at least 10 years and you haven't remarried, you may be eligible to claim on your ex-spouse's record. The ex doesn't have to be claiming yet, and your claim doesn't affect their benefit. This can substantially raise the minimum you're entitled to, especially if you had lower lifetime earnings.</p>

<h3>Do Delayed Retirement Credits keep growing past 70?</h3>

<p>No. DRCs stop accruing at age 70, so there's no financial reason to delay beyond that birthday. Your benefit continues to receive annual COLAs regardless of when you claim.</p>

<h3>How does the 2026 COLA affect my decision?</h3>

<p>It doesn't change the percentage spread between claim ages — 124% of FRA at age 70 is still 124%, with or without a 2.8% COLA. But COLAs compound on top of whatever benefit you're collecting. A larger monthly check means a larger COLA dollar amount each year, which is another subtle argument for maximizing your starting benefit if you can afford to wait.</p>

<h3>Should I claim earlier to invest the difference?</h3>

<p>Some people argue for taking benefits at 62 and investing every dollar, betting the market outperforms the 8% delayed credit. Two problems: first, you'd need a roughly 8% annual return, net of taxes and sequence-of-returns risk, just to match the guaranteed delay. Second, Social Security is an <em>inflation-indexed annuity for life</em> — a product the private market basically doesn't offer at comparable cost. Most planners view the delay premium as more valuable than the expected investment return because of those characteristics.</p>

<h3>Where can I check my exact numbers?</h3>

<p>Create a free <code>my Social Security</code> account at <a href="https://www.ssa.gov" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">ssa.gov</a> to view your actual PIA, projected benefits at every claim age, and your earnings history. That's the starting point for any serious analysis — general figures like the ones in this article are illustrative, but your PIA is specific to your 35 highest-earning years.</p>

<h2>Running your own numbers</h2>

<p>The claim-age decision isn't one-size-fits-all, but the math is knowable. Once you know your PIA from your SSA statement, plug the percentages from the table above into a retirement income plan to see how each choice interacts with your portfolio withdrawals, taxes, and spending goals. Model it in our <a href="/retirement-calculator">retirement calculator</a> alongside your 401(k) balance, and run a separate <a href="/401k-calculator">401(k) projection</a> to see how long your other assets would need to bridge you to a later claim age. The "right" age isn't the one that maximizes lifetime dollars on a spreadsheet — it's the one that best fits your cash-flow needs, your longevity, and your family situation.</p>

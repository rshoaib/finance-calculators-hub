<p>If you earn over roughly $168,000 as a single filer or $252,000 as a married couple filing jointly in 2026, the IRS tells you that you can't put a dime into a Roth IRA. But there is a perfectly legal two-step that millions of high earners use every year to fund one anyway — it's called the <strong>backdoor Roth IRA</strong>, and the math still works in 2026.</p>

<p>The gist: you contribute <em>non-deductible</em> money to a Traditional IRA (no income limit there), then convert it to a Roth (no income limit there either). The result is the same tax-free-forever growth you'd get from a direct Roth contribution. It isn't a loophole, it isn't a gray area — Congress removed the income cap on Roth conversions back in 2010, and high earners have been walking through the back door ever since.</p>

<p>This guide walks you through exactly how it works in 2026, the numbers you need to know, the one rule that trips up most people (the pro-rata rule), and how to tell whether the backdoor Roth is right for your situation.</p>

<h2>What Is a Backdoor Roth IRA?</h2>

<p>A backdoor Roth IRA isn't a separate type of account. It's a strategy — a sequence of two transactions that lets you get money into a Roth IRA when your income is too high for a direct contribution.</p>

<p>Here's the two-step in plain English:</p>

<ol>
  <li>Open a Traditional IRA (if you don't have one) and make a <strong>non-deductible</strong> contribution.</li>
  <li>Convert that Traditional IRA balance to a Roth IRA.</li>
</ol>

<p>Because the contribution was non-deductible, you don't get a tax deduction for step one. Because you already paid tax on the money before it went in, the conversion in step two is (usually) tax-free — aside from any growth that happened in between. From that point forward, the dollars grow tax-free in the Roth and come out tax-free in retirement, just like a direct Roth contribution.</p>

<p>Two things make this legal and boring rather than risky and exciting. First, there is no income limit on non-deductible Traditional IRA contributions. Second, there is no income limit on Roth conversions — the <a href="https://www.irs.gov/retirement-plans/plan-participant-employee/retirement-topics-ira-contribution-limits" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">IRS rules</a> let anyone convert, at any income, any year.</p>

<h2>How the Backdoor Roth IRA Works: Step by Step</h2>

<p>Here is the full workflow for 2026, in the order you'd actually do it.</p>

<h3>Step 1: Open a Traditional IRA</h3>

<p>If you already have one, skip this. If you don't, open a Traditional IRA at any custodian (Vanguard, Fidelity, Schwab, etc.). You'll also need a Roth IRA at the same custodian — the transfer is much simpler when both accounts live in one place.</p>

<h3>Step 2: Make a Non-Deductible Contribution</h3>

<p>Put in up to $7,500 ($8,600 if you're 50+) for 2026. Because your income is above the deduction threshold (or you're covered by a workplace plan), this contribution will be non-deductible — meaning it's after-tax money going in, and your "basis" in the IRA equals what you contributed.</p>

<p>Don't invest it in anything yet. Let it sit in the money-market sweep for a few days. Growth between contribution and conversion creates a small taxable event, so most people keep the window short.</p>

<h3>Step 3: Convert to Roth</h3>

<p>Within a week or two, initiate a Roth conversion of the full Traditional IRA balance. At most custodians this is a single click: "Convert to Roth." If your only Traditional IRA money is the $7,500 you just put in, the conversion is essentially tax-free — you already paid tax on it.</p>

<h3>Step 4: File IRS Form 8606</h3>

<p>This is the step people forget, and it matters. On your 2026 tax return (filed in 2027), your preparer or tax software should complete <strong>Form 8606</strong> — one to report the non-deductible contribution (Part I) and one to report the conversion (Part II). Form 8606 is what proves to the IRS that the converted dollars were already taxed. Miss it, and you could end up paying tax twice on the same money.</p>

<h2>A Worked Example: Single Filer Making $185,000</h2>

<p>Imagine Priya, age 38, single, with a $185,000 salary and $0 in any traditional IRA at the start of 2026. Her MAGI ($185,000) is above $168,000, so she can't contribute directly to a Roth. She does this instead:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>January 5, 2026:</strong> Opens a Traditional IRA at her brokerage and contributes $7,500 (non-deductible) for tax year 2026.</li>
  <li><strong>January 9, 2026:</strong> Converts the full $7,508.41 balance (after a few days in a 4% money-market) to her Roth IRA. The $8.41 of growth is ordinary income on her 2026 return.</li>
  <li><strong>April 2027:</strong> Files Form 8606 reporting a $7,500 non-deductible contribution and a $7,508 conversion with $7,500 of basis.</li>
</ul>

<p>Tax owed on the conversion: roughly $2 (the federal tax on $8 at her marginal rate). For a near-zero tax bill, she just fed $7,500 into a tax-free-growth account that will, at a 7% real return over 30 years, grow to roughly $57,000 — all withdrawable tax-free in retirement.</p>

<h2>The Pro-Rata Rule: The #1 Mistake High Earners Make</h2>

<p>Here is where backdoor Roths go wrong. The IRS does <em>not</em> let you cherry-pick which IRA dollars you convert. For the pro-rata rule, all your Traditional, SEP, and SIMPLE IRAs are treated as one giant account, and every conversion pulls pre-tax and after-tax dollars out in the same proportion as the combined balance.</p>

<p>Roth IRAs, 401(k)s, and 403(b)s don't count — only IRAs. But if you have a rollover IRA from an old 401(k), or a SEP IRA from a side business, those balances drag into the math. If you're picking a retirement plan for self-employment income, our <a href="/blog/solo-401k-vs-sep-ira-self-employed-2026">Solo 401(k) vs SEP IRA guide</a> walks through why a Solo 401(k) sidesteps this trap — its balances don't aggregate into the pro-rata calculation.</p>

<h3>How Pro-Rata Is Calculated</h3>

<p>Say you have $93,000 of pre-tax money in a rollover IRA plus a fresh $7,500 non-deductible contribution. Total IRA balance: $100,500. The after-tax portion is $7,500 / $100,500 ≈ <strong>7.46%</strong>. If you convert $7,500 to Roth, only 7.46% ($560) is tax-free — the other 92.54% ($6,940) is added to your income and taxed as ordinary income.</p>

<p>If you have pre-tax IRA money and still want the backdoor to work cleanly, you have two options:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Roll the Traditional IRA into your 401(k)</strong> — if your employer's plan accepts incoming rollovers. The pro-rata formula only looks at IRAs, so 401(k) dollars are invisible to the calculation.</li>
  <li><strong>Convert the whole balance at once</strong> — accepting the tax bill in a year when your income is unusually low.</li>
</ol>

<p>If neither is an option, the backdoor Roth may not be worth the tax friction for you. Before deciding, run the tax impact through our <a href="/tax-bracket-calculator">Tax Bracket Calculator</a> so you know exactly what a partial conversion would cost.</p>

<h2>Backdoor vs Mega Backdoor Roth IRA</h2>

<p>The "mega backdoor" is the backdoor Roth's bigger, more selective cousin. It lives inside a 401(k), not an IRA, and it works only if your employer's plan offers two features: <strong>after-tax contributions beyond the $24,500 pre-tax limit</strong> and <strong>in-plan Roth conversions or in-service withdrawals</strong>.</p>

<p>When those two features are present, you can contribute up to the overall 2026 defined-contribution limit ($72,000 including employer contributions, per IRS Notice 2025-67) and then convert the after-tax portion to a Roth inside your plan or rolled out to a Roth IRA. The mechanic is the same — after-tax in, Roth out — just with a much bigger ceiling.</p>

<h2>Who Should Use a Backdoor Roth IRA (and Who Shouldn't)</h2>

<p>The backdoor is best for you if all of these are true:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Your MAGI is above the 2026 Roth phase-out (over $168,000 single / $252,000 MFJ).</li>
  <li>You have little to no pre-tax money in Traditional, SEP, or SIMPLE IRAs — or your 401(k) will accept an IRA rollover.</li>
  <li>You expect to be in a similar or higher tax bracket in retirement, which makes tax-free Roth growth more valuable than an upfront deduction.</li>
  <li>You are already maxing your 401(k) — the backdoor Roth is typically an <em>addition</em>, not a replacement, for tax-advantaged savings.</li>
</ul>

<p>The backdoor is probably not worth it if:</p>

<ul>
  <li>You have a six-figure rollover IRA and your employer's 401(k) won't accept it. The pro-rata drag cancels most of the benefit.</li>
  <li>You expect to retire in a much lower tax bracket and deducting now would actually help more than Roth growth later.</li>
  <li>You haven't captured your 401(k) match yet — that's a guaranteed 50%+ return and comes first.</li>
</ul>

<p>For a broader comparison of Roth vs Traditional logic, read our post on <a href="/blog/roth-ira-vs-traditional-ira-2026">Roth IRA vs Traditional IRA in 2026</a>.</p>

<h2>Common Backdoor Roth Mistakes to Avoid</h2>

<p>These are the errors I see most often in tax-prep and CFP forums:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Skipping Form 8606.</strong> Without it, the IRS assumes your conversion was 100% pre-tax and taxes it all. File it every year you contribute <em>or</em> convert.</li>
  <li><strong>Ignoring the pro-rata rule.</strong> If you have any pre-tax IRA balance, clean it up before you convert.</li>
  <li><strong>Investing the contribution before converting.</strong> Significant growth between steps creates an unnecessary tax bill. Keep the money in cash for the few days it takes to convert.</li>
  <li><strong>Missing the contribution deadline.</strong> You have until the tax filing deadline (April 15, 2027) to make a 2026 contribution — but the conversion happens in the calendar year you do it, so the tax treatment splits across two years if you drag your feet.</li>
  <li><strong>Doing a backdoor Roth when you could have contributed directly.</strong> If your MAGI came in lower than expected, check the phase-out table above. A direct contribution has one less moving part.</li>
  <li><strong>Confusing a conversion with a rollover.</strong> Conversions are taxable events (to the extent they're pre-tax). Rollovers generally are not.</li>
</ol>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

<h3>Is the backdoor Roth IRA legal in 2026?</h3>

<p>Yes. The strategy relies on two separate provisions Congress enacted — non-deductible Traditional IRA contributions and income-agnostic Roth conversions — and the IRS has acknowledged the sequence as permissible. Proposals to close the "loophole" surfaced in 2021 but did not pass, and no legislation has changed the underlying rules for 2026.</p>

<h3>How much can I contribute to a backdoor Roth IRA in 2026?</h3>

<p>The same as any IRA: $7,500 if you're under 50, or $8,600 if you're 50 or older. Spouses can each do their own, so a married couple can move $15,000 (or up to $17,200 if both are 50+) into Roth IRAs this way.</p>

<h3>Do I have to wait before converting?</h3>

<p>There's no IRS-mandated waiting period. The old "step transaction doctrine" concern was effectively laid to rest when Congress blessed the strategy in the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act conference report. Most practitioners still recommend a short gap (a few days to a couple of weeks) just to show separate transactions and avoid growth-related tax noise.</p>

<h3>What if I already have a large Traditional IRA balance?</h3>

<p>Consider rolling it into your current 401(k) first, if your plan accepts incoming rollovers. Once your Traditional IRA balance is $0 on December 31 of the conversion year, the pro-rata rule has nothing to allocate against, and your backdoor Roth converts cleanly.</p>

<h3>Can I undo a Roth conversion?</h3>

<p>No. Recharacterizations of Roth conversions were eliminated starting in tax year 2018 by the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act. Once the conversion is done, it's done — so confirm the numbers before you click.</p>

<h3>Do I owe state income tax on a Roth conversion?</h3>

<p>Usually yes, to the extent the conversion is taxable federally. A few states (like Pennsylvania) treat IRA distributions more favorably, but most tax conversions as ordinary income. Check your state's rules or consult a local CPA before converting a large pre-tax balance.</p>

<h3>Can my spouse do a backdoor Roth even if they don't work?</h3>

<p>Yes, through a spousal IRA. If one spouse has earned income and you file jointly, the non-working spouse can still fund a Traditional IRA up to the full limit and convert it to Roth the same way.</p>

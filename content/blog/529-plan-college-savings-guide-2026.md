<p>If a baby born in 2026 starts kindergarten with $0 saved and ends up at a four-year public university in 2044, their parents could be staring at a six-figure tuition bill. A 529 plan is the most tax-efficient way most U.S. families can chip away at that number — and the rules just got friendlier. Starting January 1, 2026, the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (H.R. 1) doubles the K-12 tuition limit and opens 529s to a wider set of credentialing programs, while the SECURE 2.0 rollover lets unused balances roll into a Roth IRA. Here's how a 529 plan actually works in 2026, what the new rules mean, and the math behind starting one.</p>

<h2>What a 529 Plan Actually Is</h2>

<p>A 529 plan is a state-sponsored, tax-advantaged investment account designed for education expenses. Anyone — parent, grandparent, friend, or the future student themselves — can open one. You name a beneficiary, choose investments (usually age-based portfolios that get more conservative as the beneficiary gets closer to school), and contribute money you've already paid taxes on.</p>

<p>The account grows on a tax-deferred basis. When the money comes out for qualified education expenses, the earnings are <strong>federally tax-free</strong>. Many states layer their own deduction or credit on top of that for residents who use the in-state plan. Two flavors exist: <em>education savings plans</em> (the typical investment account most people mean by "529") and <em>prepaid tuition plans</em>, which lock in tuition rates at participating schools. This guide focuses on the savings version, which is what most families use.</p>

<h2>The Tax Benefits, Stacked</h2>

<p>The headline benefit is simple: pay tax on the dollars going in, then pay no federal tax on growth or qualified withdrawals. That's the same treatment as a Roth account, but with two important differences. First, there's <strong>no federal income limit</strong> — high earners can fund a 529 at full speed, unlike a Roth IRA. Second, contributions count as completed gifts to the beneficiary for estate-planning purposes, which lets grandparents move money out of their taxable estate while still keeping control of the account.</p>

<p>Most states sweeten the deal further. Over 30 states offer a state income tax deduction or credit for in-state 529 contributions, and a handful (Colorado, New Mexico, South Carolina, West Virginia) allow an unlimited deduction. Always check your state's rules — using your home state's plan is usually the simplest way to capture the deduction, but a few states (like Pennsylvania, Arizona, Kansas, Maine, Missouri, Montana) give the tax break on contributions to <em>any</em> state's plan.</p>

<h2>2026 Contribution Limits and the Gift Tax</h2>

<p>Unlike a 401(k) or IRA, a 529 has no federal annual contribution limit. Instead, contributions interact with the federal gift tax system. For 2026, the IRS set the <strong>annual gift tax exclusion at $19,000 per recipient</strong> ($38,000 if a married couple gifts jointly), unchanged from 2025 (Source: IRS Notice 2025-67, October 2025). You can contribute up to that amount per beneficiary per year without filing a gift tax return or chipping away at your lifetime exemption.</p>

<p>Each state also sets a maximum aggregate balance — the lifetime cap on the total balance that account(s) for a single beneficiary can hold. Most states fall between roughly $235,000 and $575,000. Once an account hits the cap, you can't add more, but earnings can keep compounding above it.</p>

<p>For perspective: $19,000 a year is plenty for the vast majority of families. The bigger question is usually how to make consistent contributions stick — automatic transfers tend to outperform "I'll do it when I have extra" by a wide margin.</p>

<h2>Superfunding: Five Years of Gifts in One</h2>

<p>The 529 has a unique gift-tax escape hatch called <strong>five-year forward gifting</strong>, often called superfunding. You can drop in up to 5× the annual exclusion in a single year and elect to spread it over five years for gift-tax purposes. In 2026, that's <strong>up to $95,000 per donor per beneficiary</strong> ($190,000 for a couple) without using any lifetime exemption (Source: <a href="https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/10-rules-for-superfunding-a-529-plan" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Saving for College</a>, 2026 superfunding rules).</p>

<p>Why this matters: getting a big lump sum compounding 18 years before college is a massive head start. The catch is you need to file <a href="https://www.irs.gov/forms-pubs/about-form-709" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">IRS Form 709</a> in the first year to make the election, and you can't make additional gifts to the same beneficiary in the next five years without eating into the exclusion.</p>

<h3>When Superfunding Makes Sense</h3>

<ul>
  <li>Grandparents wanting to reduce their taxable estate while keeping control of the funds.</li>
  <li>Parents who just received a windfall (inheritance, business sale, signing bonus) and want maximum tax-free growth time.</li>
  <li>Anyone who can comfortably afford the lump sum without disrupting retirement savings or emergency reserves.</li>
</ul>

<h2>What Counts as a Qualified Expense in 2026</h2>

<p>"Qualified expenses" determine whether withdrawals come out federally tax-free. The list expanded meaningfully effective January 1, 2026, under the One Big Beautiful Bill Act (Source: <a href="https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/529-plans-questions-and-answers" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">IRS, 529 Plans Q&amp;A</a>; <a href="https://my529.org/2025/10/qualified-education-expenses-expand-with-recent-legislation/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">my529, October 2025</a>).</p>

<table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Education Level</th><th>Qualified Expenses (2026)</th><th>Annual Limit</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>K-12 (public, private, religious)</td><td>Tuition, plus new for 2026: books, curriculum materials, online learning, standardized test fees (AP, SAT, ACT), tutoring by an unrelated qualified tutor, dual-enrollment fees, educational therapies for students with disabilities.</td><td><strong>$20,000 per beneficiary</strong> (up from $10,000)</td></tr>
    <tr><td>College / Postsecondary</td><td>Tuition and required fees, books and supplies, computers and software, room and board (if at least half-time enrollment), special-needs services.</td><td>No annual cap; limited by the beneficiary's actual qualified expenses.</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Postsecondary credentialing (new in 2026)</td><td>Tuition, fees, books, equipment, and certification testing for recognized programs — welding, aviation mechanics, IT certifications, and similar industry credentials.</td><td>No annual cap; limited by actual expenses.</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Apprenticeships</td><td>Fees, books, supplies, and equipment for registered apprenticeship programs.</td><td>No annual cap.</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Student loans</td><td>Principal and interest payments on federal or private student loans.</td><td><strong>$10,000 lifetime per beneficiary</strong> (plus $10,000 for each sibling).</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

<p>One important caveat: not every state has conformed to the expanded federal definition. Some states still tax the new K-12 uses or the credentialing expansion at the state level, even though federal treatment is tax-free. Check your state's plan website before pulling money for a non-traditional use.</p>

<p>Withdrawals for non-qualified expenses are taxed differently. The original contributions come out tax-free (you already paid tax on them). The earnings portion of a non-qualified withdrawal is subject to ordinary income tax <strong>plus a 10% federal penalty</strong>. There are exceptions — the penalty is waived if the beneficiary receives a scholarship (up to the scholarship amount), attends a U.S. military academy, becomes disabled, or dies.</p>

<h2>The 529-to-Roth IRA Rollover (SECURE 2.0)</h2>

<p>What if your beneficiary doesn't use all the money? Until recently, the answer was either change beneficiaries to another family member or eat the 10% penalty. SECURE 2.0 added a third option: <strong>roll up to $35,000 lifetime from a 529 to a Roth IRA in the beneficiary's name</strong> (Source: <a href="https://www.savingforcollege.com/article/roll-over-529-plan-funds-to-a-roth-ira" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Saving for College, 529-to-Roth Rollover Rules 2026</a>).</p>

<h3>Rollover Rules at a Glance</h3>

<ul>
  <li>The 529 account must be at least <strong>15 years old</strong>.</li>
  <li>Rollovers can't include any contributions (or earnings on those contributions) made in the <strong>last 5 years</strong>.</li>
  <li>The Roth IRA must be in the <strong>beneficiary's name</strong>, not the account owner's.</li>
  <li>The beneficiary must have <strong>earned income</strong> at least equal to the rollover amount that year.</li>
  <li>Each year's rollover counts against the beneficiary's annual Roth IRA contribution limit — <strong>$7,500 in 2026</strong>, or $8,600 if age 50+ (Source: <a href="https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/401k-limit-increases-to-24500-for-2026-ira-limit-increases-to-7500" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">IRS, Notice 2025-67</a>).</li>
  <li>Roth IRA <em>income</em> limits do <strong>not</strong> apply to these rollovers — high earners benefit too.</li>
  <li>Hitting the $35,000 lifetime cap typically takes <strong>5+ years</strong> at the annual contribution limit.</li>
</ul>

<p>This makes 529s feel less risky to overfund. Even if the beneficiary skips college, gets a big scholarship, or finishes school with money left over, that surplus can seed a tax-free retirement account.</p>

<h2>A Worked Example: Saving From Birth</h2>

<p>Imagine Maya is born in April 2026. Her parents open a 529 the same month and set up automatic contributions of <strong>$300/month</strong> ($3,600/year). They use a moderate-risk age-based portfolio that averages <strong>6% annual return</strong> after fees. They never increase the contribution.</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Total contributed over 18 years:</strong> $3,600 × 18 = <strong>$64,800</strong>.</li>
  <li><strong>Projected balance at age 18</strong> (6% compounded monthly): roughly <strong>$117,000</strong>.</li>
  <li><strong>Earnings (tax-free if used for qualified expenses):</strong> ~$52,000.</li>
  <li><strong>Federal tax saved on those earnings</strong> at a 22% bracket: roughly $11,400 — money that would have been lost in a taxable account.</li>
</ol>

<p>If Maya ends up with a scholarship and only spends $80,000 of the balance, her parents have options: leave it for graduate school, change the beneficiary to a sibling, withdraw with the scholarship-penalty exception, or — if the account is old enough — roll up to $35,000 into a Roth IRA in Maya's name over time, giving her a head start on retirement.</p>

<h2>Common Mistakes to Avoid</h2>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Funding a 529 before an emergency fund or retirement.</strong> You can borrow for college; you can't borrow for retirement. Cover those bases first.</li>
  <li><strong>Using your home state's plan reflexively.</strong> If your state offers no income tax deduction (or has tiny limits), a low-cost out-of-state plan with better fund options may net more after taxes.</li>
  <li><strong>Picking aggressive investments close to enrollment.</strong> A 100% stock allocation when the beneficiary is 17 can wipe out years of growth in a single bad year. Age-based portfolios automatically de-risk.</li>
  <li><strong>Ignoring fees.</strong> A 0.5% expense ratio gap on a 529 plan compounded over 18 years can cost thousands. Compare expense ratios on Morningstar's annual plan ratings before you pick.</li>
  <li><strong>Forgetting state recapture rules.</strong> Some states recapture deductions if you roll the account out of state or use it for non-qualified expenses. Check before moving funds.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

<h3>Can I open a 529 for a child who isn't mine?</h3>
<p>Yes. The account owner doesn't have to be a parent. Grandparents, aunts and uncles, godparents, and even unrelated adults can open accounts. The owner controls investments and withdrawals; the beneficiary doesn't have automatic access at age 18 the way they would with a UGMA/UTMA.</p>

<h3>What happens if my child gets a full scholarship?</h3>
<p>You can withdraw an amount equal to the scholarship without the 10% penalty (you'll still owe ordinary income tax on the earnings portion). Or change the beneficiary to a sibling, cousin, or yourself, leave the money for graduate school, or — if the account is at least 15 years old — roll up to $35,000 into a Roth IRA in the original beneficiary's name over multiple years.</p>

<h3>How does a 529 affect financial aid?</h3>
<p>A parent-owned 529 is reported as a parent asset on the FAFSA, which reduces aid eligibility by no more than 5.64% of the account balance. A grandparent-owned 529 used to count harshly, but recent FAFSA simplification removed the impact of grandparent-owned 529 distributions on federal aid eligibility. Private colleges using the CSS Profile may still consider them.</p>

<h3>Can I change the beneficiary?</h3>
<p>Yes, and easily. You can change the beneficiary to another "member of the family" (broadly defined — siblings, parents, first cousins, in-laws) without tax consequences. This flexibility is one of the strongest reasons to start funding early without worrying about which child or grandchild will need it most.</p>

<h3>What if I overfund the account?</h3>
<p>Options include keeping it for graduate school, rolling beneficiaries to a relative, withdrawing under one of the penalty exceptions, paying up to $10,000 of student loans per beneficiary, or rolling up to $35,000 lifetime to a Roth IRA in the beneficiary's name (subject to the 15-year and 5-year rules above). For high-income parents thinking about backdoor Roth strategies, see our <a href="/blog/backdoor-roth-ira-2026-high-earners-guide">2026 backdoor Roth IRA guide</a>.</p>

<h3>Is a 529 better than a Roth IRA for college?</h3>
<p>It depends on whether you'll use the money for education. The 529 is more tax-efficient for qualified education expenses (no income tax on earnings, no penalty). The Roth gives you flexibility — if your child doesn't need the money, you keep it for retirement. Many families fund both, prioritizing the Roth if there's room and the 529 once retirement contributions are on track. Compare the trade-offs in our <a href="/blog/roth-ira-vs-traditional-ira-2026">Roth vs. traditional IRA guide</a>.</p>

<h3>Are 529 contributions deductible on federal taxes?</h3>
<p>No. Contributions are made with after-tax dollars at the federal level. The federal benefit shows up as tax-free growth and qualified withdrawals. The state-level deduction or credit, where it exists, is what you get up front in the contribution year.</p>

<p>If you freelance, run a side hustle, or own a small business, no employer is quietly withholding taxes from each paycheck. The IRS still wants its money — just in four chunks instead of 26. Miss those quarterly payments and you can rack up an underpayment penalty that compounds daily, even if you eventually pay the right total when you file.</p>

<p>The next deadline is the one that catches people: <strong>Q2 2026 estimated taxes are due Monday, June 15, 2026</strong>. This guide walks through who has to pay, the 2026 due dates, the safe-harbor rules that protect you from penalties, and a worked example showing how a freelancer earning $90,000 actually calculates their quarterly check.</p>

<h2>Who Has to Pay Quarterly Estimated Taxes in 2026?</h2>

<p>The IRS rule of thumb is simple: if you expect to owe at least <strong>$1,000</strong> in federal tax for 2026 after subtracting any withholding and refundable credits, you generally need to make quarterly estimated payments. That threshold is low — at the 2026 self-employment plus federal income tax rates, you can hit it on roughly $5,000–$6,000 of net self-employment profit.</p>

<p>You can also skip estimated payments if your employer withholding (from a W-2 day job, a spouse's job filed jointly, or pension/Social Security withholding) covers the smaller of:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>90%</strong> of your 2026 total tax, or</li>
  <li><strong>100%</strong> of your 2025 total tax (or <strong>110%</strong> if your 2025 AGI was over $150,000, or over $75,000 if married filing separately).</li>
</ul>

<p>That second rule is the one most people aim for. It's mechanical: look at line 24 of last year's Form 1040, multiply by 1.0 (or 1.1 if your AGI was high), and divide by four. We'll walk through it below.</p>

<p>Common groups who owe estimated taxes: 1099 freelancers and contractors, sole proprietors and single-member LLCs, partners in partnerships and S-corp shareholders, gig workers (rideshare, delivery, marketplace platforms), landlords with positive rental income, and retirees taking large IRA distributions without enough withholding.</p>

<p>You can also skip the Q4 payment entirely if you file your 2026 Form 1040 and pay the balance due by January 31, 2027. That's a useful trick if your income is uneven and you want to wait until you have full-year numbers.</p>

<h2>How Self-Employment Tax Stacks on Top of Income Tax</h2>

<p>Self-employed workers pay two federal taxes on the same dollar of profit: regular income tax (which moves with your tax bracket) and <strong>self-employment (SE) tax</strong> at a flat 15.3%. SE tax is the freelancer version of the FICA payroll tax — it funds Social Security and Medicare. When you have a W-2 employer, your employer pays half (7.65%) and you pay half (7.65%). When you're self-employed, you pay both halves yourself.</p>

<p>SE tax breaks down into:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>12.4% Social Security</strong>, applied to the first $184,500 of net self-employment earnings in 2026 (the 2026 Social Security wage base, per the <a href="https://www.ssa.gov/oact/cola/cbb.html" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">SSA Contribution and Benefit Base</a>). Maximum Social Security portion of SE tax in 2026 = 12.4% × $184,500 = $22,878.</li>
  <li><strong>2.9% Medicare</strong>, applied to all net self-employment earnings — no cap.</li>
</ul>

<p>Two important wrinkles. First, you only pay SE tax on <strong>92.35%</strong> of your net profit (the IRS lets you exclude the deemed "employer half" before computing SE tax). Second, you get to deduct half of your SE tax as an above-the-line adjustment to income, which reduces your federal income tax bill (but not your SE tax). For details see the <a href="https://www.irs.gov/businesses/small-businesses-self-employed/self-employment-tax-social-security-and-medicare-taxes" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">IRS Self-Employment Tax page</a>.</p>

<p>The practical implication: a freelancer in the 22% federal bracket is effectively paying roughly <em>22% income tax + 14.13% net SE tax</em> on incremental dollars (the "effective" SE rate after the deduction). Add a 5% state tax and you're at ~41% marginal — which is why setting aside 25–35% of every invoice is a common rule of thumb.</p>

<h2>The Safe Harbor: Avoiding the Underpayment Penalty</h2>

<p>"Safe harbor" doesn't mean you nailed your tax bill exactly. It means you stayed inside one of three guardrails, so the IRS will not charge an underpayment penalty even if you still owe a big check at filing time. Pick whichever is easiest for your situation:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>The "owe less than $1,000" rule.</strong> If you'll owe under $1,000 at filing time, you don't need estimated payments at all.</li>
  <li><strong>The 90%-of-current-year rule.</strong> Pay at least 90% of your actual 2026 tax through withholding plus estimated payments. Best if your income drops year over year.</li>
  <li><strong>The 100%/110% prior-year rule.</strong> Pay at least 100% of last year's total tax (110% if your 2025 AGI exceeded $150,000, or $75,000 if married filing separately). This is the easiest and most popular path — no forecasting required.</li>
</ol>

<p>The penalty itself is calculated like interest: the IRS multiplies your shortfall for each quarter by the federal short-term rate plus 3%, accruing daily. Penalty rates change quarterly. The takeaway: even one missed quarter can cost you, and the only way to undo it is to pay early in the next quarter to stop the meter.</p>

<h2>A Worked Example: Maya, the $90,000 Freelance Designer</h2>

<p>Maya is a single-filer freelance designer based in Texas (no state income tax). Her 2025 Form 1040 line 24 (total tax) was <strong>$15,400</strong>. She expects similar income in 2026, around $90,000 of net profit, and has no W-2 withholding.</p>

<p>Her 2025 AGI was below $150,000, so the prior-year safe harbor is 100% × $15,400 = <strong>$15,400</strong> for 2026. Divided by four:</p>

<p>If Maya sends $3,850 every quarter, she's safe-harbored for 2026 — even if her income jumps and she ends up owing $20,000 total. She'll write a check for the difference at filing time, but no penalty.</p>

<p>To estimate the same number from scratch (the 90%-of-current-year approach), she would: estimate net profit ($90,000), subtract half of SE tax (~$6,358), subtract the 2026 standard deduction for single filers ($16,100, per the <a href="https://www.irs.gov/newsroom/irs-releases-tax-inflation-adjustments-for-tax-year-2026-including-amendments-from-the-one-big-beautiful-bill" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">IRS 2026 inflation adjustments</a>), apply the 2026 brackets, then add back full SE tax (~$12,716). <h2>How to Actually Send the Payment</h2>

<p>The fastest free options are direct bank-account payments through the IRS:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>IRS Direct Pay</strong> — one-time payments from a checking or savings account, no enrollment required. Reason code: "Estimated Tax 1040ES."</li>
  <li><strong>EFTPS</strong> (Electronic Federal Tax Payment System) — requires enrollment but lets you schedule payments months in advance and view full payment history. Useful for businesses with payroll obligations.</li>
  <li><strong>IRS2Go mobile app</strong> — same Direct Pay rails, easier on a phone.</li>
  <li><strong>Mailing Form 1040-ES vouchers</strong> — still works, but slow and easy to lose. Use certified mail if you go this route.</li>
  <li><strong>Credit/debit card</strong> via approved processors — convenient but adds a ~1.85%–1.98% fee. Only worth it if you're chasing card rewards that exceed the fee.</li>
</ul>

<p>Whichever method you pick, save the confirmation number. You'll plug those amounts into Schedule 3 / Line 26 of your Form 1040 next April.</p>

<h2>Five Common Mistakes That Trigger Penalties</h2>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Treating quarters as calendar quarters.</strong> Q2 covers two months (April–May), not three. Many people underpay Q2 because they assume they have until June 30.</li>
  <li><strong>Forgetting state estimated taxes.</strong> Most states with an income tax have parallel quarterly deadlines, often the same dates. Skipping state estimateds can mean a separate state-level penalty.</li>
  <li><strong>Spending the tax money.</strong> A separate "tax" savings account moved at every invoice deposit (25–35% of each payment) prevents the scramble. A high-yield savings account works well — see our piece on a <a href="/blog/hysa-vs-cd-savings-2026">HYSA vs CD comparison</a> for where to park it.</li>
  <li><strong>Ignoring the additional Medicare tax.</strong> If your combined wages plus SE earnings exceed $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married filing jointly), an extra 0.9% Medicare surtax kicks in on the excess — and you're expected to estimate it yourself.</li>
  <li><strong>Confusing the safe-harbor floor with what you'll actually owe.</strong> Safe harbor stops penalties; it doesn't mean you've paid in full. If your income jumps mid-year, you'll still write a balance-due check at filing time. Banking that money along the way is on you.</li>
</ol>

<h2>What About State Estimated Taxes?</h2>

<p>Forty-three U.S. states and Washington D.C. levy a personal income tax, and most require quarterly estimated payments under their own (sometimes different) safe-harbor rules. Common variations: California's June payment is 40% of the annual estimate, not 25%; New York and New Jersey have their own coupon books; some states like Pennsylvania use a flat 3.07% rate so the math is much simpler.</p>

<p>Check your state's department of revenue site and look up "estimated tax" for the current-year voucher. The federal due dates are a reasonable cue to also send the state payment — paying both at once is the easiest way not to forget.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

<h3>What happens if I just pay nothing all year and settle at filing time?</h3>
<p>You'll owe the full balance plus an underpayment penalty calculated quarter by quarter. The penalty rate has hovered around 7%–8% APR in recent years, applied to whatever shortfall existed at each due date. On a $15,000 unpaid balance, that's roughly $300–$600 in extra cost — preventable by sending the payments along the way.</p>

<h3>Do I need to send equal payments each quarter?</h3>
<p>Not necessarily. The default safe-harbor calculation assumes equal quarterly payments, but if your income is seasonal (heavy in Q3, light in Q1), you can use the <strong>annualized income installment method</strong> (Form 2210, Schedule AI) to match payments to actual earnings. It's more paperwork but reduces overpayment in slow quarters.</p>

<h3>Can I just increase W-2 withholding from a side job and skip estimated payments?</h3>
<p>Yes — and it's often a smarter approach for couples filing jointly with one W-2 spouse. Withholding is treated as paid evenly throughout the year, regardless of when you actually pay it, so a December bonus with extra federal withholding can fix a Q1 underpayment retroactively. Adjust via Form W-4.</p>

<h3>What if my income is wildly uncertain — say, my first year freelancing?</h3>
<p>The 100%-of-prior-year rule still works, even if last year was mostly W-2 wages. If line 24 of your 2025 Form 1040 was $7,000, paying $1,750/quarter in 2026 keeps you in safe harbor regardless of what you actually earn. Then settle up at filing time.</p>

<h3>Are estimated tax payments deductible?</h3>
<p>The estimated tax payment itself is not a deduction — it's a prepayment of the tax you'll calculate on Form 1040. But half of the SE tax inside that bill is deductible (it shows up on Schedule 1, line 15), as are eligible business expenses on Schedule C, the qualified business income (QBI) deduction, retirement contributions through a SEP-IRA or Solo 401(k), and self-employed health insurance.</p>

<h3>Do I owe estimated taxes on my Roth IRA conversion or capital gains?</h3>
<p>Yes — anything that creates taxable income for the year flows into the same calculation. Big one-time events (a Roth conversion, an investment sale, an inheritance distribution) can blow past the prior-year safe harbor in a single quarter. Use the annualized installment method or just bump up the next estimated payment to cover the spike. See our explainer on <a href="/blog/capital-gains-tax-short-term-vs-long-term">short-term vs long-term capital gains</a> for the rate side of that math.</p>

<h3>What if I miss a deadline by a few days?</h3>
<p>Pay as soon as you realize. The penalty accrues daily, so a payment made on June 20 instead of June 15 only costs the proportional penalty for those five days — not a full quarter. Don't wait for "the next quarter" to fix it; the cost grows every day.</p>

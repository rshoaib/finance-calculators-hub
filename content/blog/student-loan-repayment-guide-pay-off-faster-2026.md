Whether you just graduated or have been chipping away for years, understanding your options can save you <strong>thousands of dollars</strong> in interest.</p>

<h2>How Student Loan Interest Works</h2>

<p>Student loans use <strong>simple daily interest</strong>, meaning interest accrues on your outstanding principal every single day. The daily interest formula:</p>

<p><strong>Daily Interest = Principal Balance × (Interest Rate ÷ 365)</strong></p>

<p>For a <strong>$35,000 loan at 5.5%</strong>, that's roughly <strong>$5.27 per day</strong> — or about $160 per month in interest alone at the start of repayment. This is why paying early and paying extra has such a dramatic compounding effect.</p>

<h3>Grace Period Capitalization — The Hidden Cost</h3>

<p>Most federal loans come with a 6-month grace period after graduation. During this time, <strong>unsubsidized loans still accrue interest</strong>. If you skip payments during grace:</p>

<ul>
<li>$35,000 at 5.5% → <strong>$962 in grace period interest</strong></li>
<li>That $962 gets <strong>capitalized</strong> (added to your principal), so your balance becomes $35,962</li>
<li>You then pay interest on the larger balance for the <strong>entire 10-year repayment</strong></li>
</ul>

<p><strong>What this means for you:</strong> Even making interest-only payments during grace ($160/month) prevents capitalization and saves you hundreds over the life of the loan. Use our <a href="/student-loan-calculator">Student Loan Calculator</a> to see the exact impact.</p>

<h3>What Changed in 2026?</h3>

<ul>
<li><strong>SAVE plan terminated:</strong> Following a December 2025 settlement, the SAVE plan has ended. Current SAVE borrowers must transition to IBR or RAP by July 2028.</li>
<li><strong>New RAP plan:</strong> The Repayment Assistance Plan replaces most IDR plans for loans issued after July 1, 2026 — payments range from 1% to 10% of AGI.</li>
<li><strong>PAYE and ICR phasing out:</strong> Both plans will be eliminated for all borrowers by July 1, 2028.</li>
<li><strong>Forgiveness may be taxable again:</strong> The American Rescue Act exemption expired at the end of 2025 — IDR forgiveness could be treated as taxable income.</li>
<li><strong>Parent PLUS changes:</strong> Parents who don't consolidate before July 2026 lose access to income-driven repayment permanently.</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>What this means for you:</strong> If you have loans disbursed before July 2026 and want income-driven repayment, enroll in IBR now while it is still available. New borrowers after July 2026 will only have Standard and RAP.</p>

<h2>7 Strategies to Pay Off Student Loans Faster</h2>

<h3>1. Pay Interest During Your Grace Period</h3>
<p>Even $160/month during the 6-month grace period prevents capitalization and saves you hundreds. On a $35,000 unsubsidized loan, skipping grace payments adds $962 to your balance — and you pay interest on that extra amount for 10 years.</p>

<h3>2. Make Biweekly Payments</h3>
<p>Instead of one monthly payment, pay half every two weeks. Because there are 26 biweekly periods per year (not 24), you end up making <strong>13 full payments annually</strong> instead of 12 — without feeling the difference. This alone can shave 1-2 years off a 10-year loan.</p>

<h3>3. Use the Debt Avalanche Method</h3>
<p>If you have multiple loans, list them by interest rate (highest first). Make minimum payments on all loans, then throw every extra dollar at the highest-rate loan. Once it is paid off, roll that payment into the next highest. This minimizes total interest paid. Track your progress with our <a href="/debt-payoff-calculator">Debt Payoff Calculator</a>.</p>

<h3>4. Set Up Autopay for the 0.25% Discount</h3>
<p>Nearly every federal and private servicer offers a <strong>0.25% interest rate reduction</strong> when you enroll in autopay. On a $35,000 loan, that saves roughly $500 over 10 years — for doing nothing.</p>

<h3>5. Apply Windfalls Directly to Principal</h3>
<p>Tax refunds, work bonuses, birthday money — applying a $2,000 lump sum to principal early in your repayment can save $800+ in interest. Always specify to your servicer that extra payments should be applied to <strong>principal, not future payments</strong>.</p>

<h3>6. Consider Refinancing (With Caution)</h3>
<p>If your credit score has improved since graduation, you might qualify for a lower rate through refinancing. A 2% reduction on $35,000 saves over $4,000 in interest. But <strong>never refinance federal loans into private loans</strong> if you need income-driven plans, PSLF eligibility, or deferment/forbearance protections.</p>

<h3>7. Round Up Your Payments</h3>
<p>If your payment is $380, round up to $400. That extra $20/month adds up to $2,400 over 10 years in extra principal payments and can shave months off your loan. Check the exact impact with our <a href="/student-loan-calculator">Student Loan Calculator</a>.</p>

<h2>When Does PSLF Make Sense?</h2>

<p>Public Service Loan Forgiveness forgives your remaining federal loan balance after <strong>120 qualifying payments</strong> (10 years) while working for a qualifying employer (government, nonprofit, 501(c)(3)). PSLF forgiveness remains <strong>tax-free</strong> even after the 2025 exemption expiration.</p>

<p>PSLF makes financial sense when:</p>
<ul>
<li>Your loan balance is <strong>more than your annual salary</strong></li>
<li>You plan to stay in public service for <strong>at least 10 years</strong></li>
<li>You are on an income-driven plan with <strong>low monthly payments</strong></li>
</ul>

<p>If your payments are so low that you will pay less over 10 years than you owe, PSLF saves you money. If your income is high enough that you would pay off the loan within 10 years anyway, PSLF offers no benefit. Model both scenarios with our <a href="/student-loan-calculator">Student Loan Calculator</a>.</p>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

<h3>What is the average student loan balance in 2026?</h3>
<p>The average federal student loan balance is approximately <strong>$38,000</strong>, with total education debt (including private loans) averaging higher. Monthly payments on the standard 10-year plan for this amount typically run $400-$420 at current rates.</p>

<h3>Should I pay off student loans or invest?</h3>
<p>Compare your student loan interest rate to expected investment returns. If your loans are above 6%, prioritize paying them off — guaranteed 6% return via interest savings beats uncertain market returns. Below 4%, investing typically wins. Between 4-6%, consider splitting your extra cash. Use our <a href="/compound-interest-calculator">Compound Interest Calculator</a> to compare growth scenarios.</p>

<h3>Can I deduct student loan interest on my taxes?</h3>
<p>Yes. You can deduct up to <strong>$2,500 in student loan interest</strong> per year on your federal tax return, even if you don't itemize. The deduction phases out at higher income levels ($80K-$95K single, $165K-$195K married filing jointly for 2026). Use our <a href="/tax-bracket-calculator">Tax Bracket Calculator</a> to estimate your savings.</p>

<h3>What happens if I can't afford my student loan payments?</h3>
<p>Contact your servicer immediately. Options include income-driven repayment plans (IBR or the new RAP), deferment, or forbearance. Never simply stop paying — defaulting damages your credit score and can lead to wage garnishment. Income-driven plans can reduce payments to as low as $0/month based on your income.</p>

<h3>Is student loan refinancing worth it?</h3>
<p>Refinancing is worth it when you can get a rate at least 1% lower than your current rate, have stable income, and don't need federal protections (income-driven plans, PSLF, deferment). A 2% rate reduction on $35,000 saves approximately $4,000 over a 10-year term. But refinancing federal loans into private loans permanently eliminates all federal benefits.</p>

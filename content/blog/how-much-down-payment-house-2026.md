<p>Ask ten people how much you need for a house down payment, and most will say "20%." That number is <strong>outdated</strong>. In 2026, the <em>actual</em> minimum ranges from <strong>0% to 20%</strong> depending on your loan type, credit score, and the property you're buying.</p>

<p>The average first-time homebuyer in the US puts down about <strong>6-7%</strong>. And if you qualify for a VA or USDA loan, you might not need <em>any</em> down payment at all.</p>

<p>Let's break down exactly what you need — no guesswork, no jargon.</p>

<h2>Conventional Loans: The 3% to 20% Spectrum</h2>

<p>Conventional mortgages are the most common type, accounting for roughly 70% of all mortgages. Here's how the down payment works:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>3% down</strong> — Available through programs like Fannie Mae's HomeReady or Freddie Mac's Home Possible. You'll need a credit score of 620+ and will pay Private Mortgage Insurance (PMI) until you hit 20% equity.</li>
<li><strong>5-10% down</strong> — Still requires PMI but lowers your monthly payment compared to 3%.</li>
<li><strong>20% down</strong> — Eliminates PMI entirely and often qualifies you for the best interest rates.</li>
</ul>

<h2>FHA Loans: 3.5% Down (or 10%)</h2>

<p>FHA loans are backed by the Federal Housing Administration and are designed for buyers who don't qualify for conventional financing:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Credit score 580+</strong> → 3.5% minimum down payment</li>
<li><strong>Credit score 500-579</strong> → 10% minimum down payment</li>
</ul>

<p>The trade-off? FHA loans come with <strong>Mortgage Insurance Premium (MIP)</strong> — an upfront fee of 1.75% of the loan amount plus an annual premium of 0.55% that lasts the life of the loan (unless you refinance into a conventional loan later).</p>

<p><strong>When FHA makes sense:</strong> If your credit score is between 580-660 and you have limited savings, FHA is often the fastest path to homeownership.</p>

<h2>VA Loans: $0 Down for Veterans</h2>

<p>If you're an eligible veteran, active-duty service member, or surviving spouse, VA loans are arguably the best mortgage product available:</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Zero down payment required</strong></li>
<li><strong>No PMI ever</strong></li>
<li><strong>Competitive interest rates</strong> (often 0.25-0.5% lower than conventional)</li>
<li>A one-time <strong>VA funding fee</strong> (1.25-3.3% depending on usage and down payment) that can be rolled into the loan</li>
</ul>

<p>While you <em>can</em> buy with $0 down, making even a small down payment reduces the funding fee and lowers your monthly payment.</p>

<h2>USDA Loans: $0 Down in Rural/Suburban Areas</h2>

<p>USDA loans offer 100% financing — no down payment required — for buyers in eligible rural and suburban areas. You'd be surprised by what qualifies: many areas just outside major cities are USDA-eligible.</p>

<ul>
<li><strong>Income limits</strong> — Your household income must be at or below 115% of the area's median income.</li>
<li><strong>Location requirement</strong> — Check the <a href="https://eligibility.sc.egov.usda.gov/eligibility/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">USDA eligibility map</a> to see if your target area qualifies.</li>
<li><strong>Guarantee fee</strong> — 1% upfront + 0.35% annually (lower than FHA's MIP).</li>
</ul>

<h2>The PMI Question: Is Paying It Worth It?</h2>

<p>Private Mortgage Insurance protects the <em>lender</em> (not you) if you default. On a conventional loan, you'll pay PMI until your equity reaches 20%. Here's the real cost:</p>

<table>
<thead><tr><th>Down Payment</th><th>Estimated Monthly PMI</th><th>Years Until PMI Drops Off</th></tr></thead>
<tbody>
<tr><td>3% ($350K home)</td><td>$120-180/mo</td><td>~8-10 years</td></tr>
<tr><td>5% ($350K home)</td><td>$100-150/mo</td><td>~7-9 years</td></tr>
<tr><td>10% ($350K home)</td><td>$70-110/mo</td><td>~4-6 years</td></tr>
<tr><td>15% ($350K home)</td><td>$40-60/mo</td><td>~2-3 years</td></tr>
</tbody>
</table>

<p><strong>The math:</strong> If PMI costs $150/mo and you'd need 5 more years to save up to 20%, that's $9,000 in PMI vs. potentially $50,000+ in home equity gains in a rising market. For most first-time buyers, paying PMI and buying sooner is the smarter financial move.</p>

<h2>How to Save for a Down Payment Faster</h2>

<p>If you're still building your down payment fund, here are proven strategies:</p>

<ol>
<li><strong>Open a high-yield savings account (HYSA)</strong> — Current rates in 2026 range from 4.0-4.5% APY. On $20,000, that's $800-900 in annual interest. Compare with our <a href="/savings-goal-calculator">Savings Calculator</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Automate transfers</strong> — Set up automatic weekly transfers from checking to your down payment fund. Even $200/week adds up to $10,400/year.</li>
<li><strong>Use the 50/30/20 rule</strong> — Allocate 20% of take-home pay directly to savings. Read our <a href="/blog/50-30-20-budget-rule-explained-guide-2026">complete 50/30/20 guide</a> for details.</li>
<li><strong>Down payment assistance programs</strong> — Over 2,400 city, county, and state programs exist. Many offer grants (free money) or forgivable loans. Check <a href="https://downpaymentresource.com/" target="_blank" rel="noopener">DownPaymentResource.com</a>.</li>
<li><strong>Consider CD laddering</strong> — Lock portions of your savings in 3-month, 6-month, and 12-month CDs for higher guaranteed returns while maintaining some liquidity.</li>
</ol>

<h2>20% Down: Is It Still the Gold Standard?</h2>

<p>Putting 20% down has real advantages — but it's not always the best strategy.</p>

<h3>Pros of 20% Down</h3>
<ul>
<li>No PMI (saves $80-200/month)</li>
<li>Lower monthly payments</li>
<li>Better interest rates</li>
<li>Stronger offer in competitive markets</li>
<li>More equity protection if home values dip</li>
</ul>

<h3>When Less Than 20% Makes More Sense</h3>
<ul>
<li>Home prices are rising faster than you can save</li>
<li>Current mortgage rates are low and may increase</li>
<li>You qualify for VA or USDA (0% down, no PMI)</li>
<li>You'd drain your emergency fund to hit 20%</li>
<li>You're paying rent that exceeds what your mortgage would be</li>
</ul>

<p><strong>Bottom line:</strong> Don't let the 20% myth keep you renting for years longer than necessary. Run the numbers, and if buying with 5-10% down makes financial sense, do it.</p>

<h2>FAQ</h2>

<h3>What's the minimum down payment for a first-time homebuyer?</h3>
<p>As low as <strong>0% with a VA or USDA loan</strong>, or <strong>3% with a conventional loan</strong>, or <strong>3.5% with an FHA loan</strong>. Most first-time buyers put down between 3-7% and use PMI loans while building equity.</p>

<h3>Is a 5% down payment enough?</h3>
<p>Yes. A 5% down payment is accepted on conventional and FHA loans. You'll pay PMI, but that cost is typically $100-150/month on a median-priced home — far less than continuing to rent while saving more.</p>

<h3>How much is a down payment on a $300,000 house?</h3>
<p>It depends on your loan type: <strong>$0 (VA/USDA), $9,000 (3% conventional), $10,500 (3.5% FHA), or $60,000 (20% conventional)</strong>. Use our <a href="/mortgage-calculator">Mortgage Calculator</a> to see exactly how each option affects your monthly payment.</p>

<h3>Do I need 20% down to buy a house?</h3>
<p>No. The 20% requirement is a myth. While 20% eliminates PMI and gives you better rates, the vast majority of homebuyers put down less. Programs exist for 0%, 3%, and 3.5% down payments depending on your eligibility.</p>

<h3>What is PMI and can I avoid it?</h3>
<p>Private Mortgage Insurance is required on conventional loans with less than 20% down. It protects the lender, not you. You can avoid PMI by: putting 20% down, choosing a VA loan (no PMI ever), or using a lender-paid PMI option (which typically means a slightly higher interest rate).</p>

<p>Your portfolio just had a rough quarter, and one of your stock holdings is down 25%. Frustrating &mdash; but if you sell that loser at the right moment, the IRS will essentially write you a check. That is the core idea behind <strong>tax-loss harvesting</strong>: you turn paper losses inside a taxable brokerage account into a real reduction in this year's tax bill, while keeping your overall investment plan intact.</p>

<p>The arithmetic is straightforward, but the rules around <em>which</em> losses you can claim, in <em>what order</em> they offset gains, and the booby trap known as the <strong>wash sale rule</strong> trip up plenty of investors. This 2026 guide walks through every piece &mdash; with real numbers, the IRS-mandated netting order, the $3,000 ordinary-income cap, and a five-step playbook you can run before December 31.</p>

<h2>What is tax-loss harvesting?</h2>

<p>Tax-loss harvesting is the practice of <strong>selling an investment that has dropped in value to realize a capital loss</strong>, then using that loss to offset capital gains you have taken elsewhere in the year and to deduct up to $3,000 of ordinary income if any net loss remains. You typically reinvest the proceeds in a similar &mdash; but not "substantially identical" &mdash; security so your portfolio's overall risk and exposure stay roughly the same.</p>

<p>It only works inside <strong>taxable accounts</strong> (a regular brokerage account). Losses inside an IRA, Roth IRA, 401(k), or HSA never produce a deduction, because those accounts are already tax-sheltered. If you mostly invest through retirement plans, you have nothing to harvest.</p>

<p>Two things tax-loss harvesting is <em>not</em>:</p>

<ul>
  <li>It is not a "loss" in the everyday sense. The decline must be <strong>realized</strong> &mdash; you must actually sell. Holding a stock that is down does nothing for your taxes.</li>
  <li>It is not a way to manufacture wealth. Harvesting is a tax-timing strategy. You have not created anything new; you have shifted the tax bill from this year to a future year, when the replacement holding eventually appreciates and you sell it.</li>
</ul>

<h2>How the math works: a $10,000 loss turned into $2,400 of savings</h2>

<p>Suppose you are single, earn $130,000 of W-2 income, and sit in the 24% federal marginal bracket for 2026. During the year:</p>

<ul>
  <li>You sold one stock at a $7,000 short-term gain (held 9 months).</li>
  <li>You sold a fund at a $10,000 long-term loss.</li>
</ul>

<p>Here is what happens line by line on Schedule D:</p>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Net within categories first.</strong> No other short-term losses, so the short-term gain stays at $7,000. No other long-term gains, so the long-term loss stays at $10,000.</li>
  <li><strong>Cross the categories.</strong> The $10,000 long-term loss wipes out the $7,000 short-term gain, leaving a $3,000 net long-term loss.</li>
  <li><strong>Apply the $3,000 ordinary-income deduction.</strong> The remaining $3,000 reduces your ordinary income.</li>
</ol>

<p>The dollars-and-cents impact:</p>

<table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Line item</th><th>Without harvesting</th><th>With harvesting</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>Tax on $7,000 short-term gain (24%)</td><td>$1,680 owed</td><td>$0 (offset by loss)</td></tr>
    <tr><td>Ordinary income reduced by $3,000 at 24%</td><td>&mdash;</td><td>$720 saved</td></tr>
    <tr><td><strong>Total federal-tax difference</strong></td><td>$1,680 owed</td><td><strong>$2,400 saved</strong></td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

<p>That is a real $2,400 reduction in this year's federal tax bill, plus state savings on top in most states. To project your own outcome at your marginal rate, plug the numbers into our <a href="/capital-gains-tax-calculator">Capital Gains Tax Calculator</a> and our <a href="/tax-bracket-calculator">Tax Bracket Calculator</a>.</p>

<h2>The IRS-mandated netting order</h2>

<p>The order in which gains and losses combine is not optional. The IRS requires this sequence on Schedule D, which is why short-term and long-term transactions are tracked separately on Form 8949.</p>

<table>
  <thead>
    <tr><th>Step</th><th>Rule</th></tr>
  </thead>
  <tbody>
    <tr><td>1</td><td>Net all short-term losses against short-term gains.</td></tr>
    <tr><td>2</td><td>Net all long-term losses against long-term gains.</td></tr>
    <tr><td>3</td><td>If one category has a net loss and the other has a net gain, the net loss offsets the net gain.</td></tr>
    <tr><td>4</td><td>Any remaining net loss reduces ordinary income, capped at $3,000 ($1,500 if married filing separately).</td></tr>
    <tr><td>5</td><td>Any unused loss carries forward indefinitely, retaining its short-term or long-term character.</td></tr>
  </tbody>
</table>

<p>Why does the order matter? Short-term gains are taxed at ordinary rates (10% to 37% in 2026), while long-term gains are taxed at 0%, 15%, or 20%. So a long-term loss that ends up sheltering a short-term gain is far more valuable than the same loss sheltering a long-term gain. You do not get to pick the sequence &mdash; but understanding it helps you plan which positions to sell and which to hold.</p>

<h2>The $3,000 ordinary-income cap (and why it stings)</h2>

<p>The $3,000 annual deduction against ordinary income has not moved since it was set in 1978. According to <a href="https://www.irs.gov/taxtopics/tc409" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">IRS Topic No. 409</a>, the cap is the lesser of $3,000 or your total net loss ($1,500 if you file married-filing-separately). It is not adjusted for inflation, so its real value has eroded by roughly 80% over four-plus decades.</p>

<p>Two practical consequences:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Harvesting a loss to offset a known capital gain is much more tax-efficient than harvesting it against ordinary income, because the loss-against-gain offset is uncapped.</li>
  <li>Stockpiling huge unused losses is rarely "free money." A million dollars of carryforwards still drains at $3,000 per year against W-2 income unless you generate offsetting capital gains.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Capital loss carryforward: the unused portion lives on</h2>

<p>If your total net loss exceeds $3,000 plus your gains, the IRS lets you carry the excess forward to <strong>future tax years indefinitely</strong>, with no expiration. The carried-forward amount keeps its character &mdash; short-term losses remain short-term losses, long-term losses remain long-term losses &mdash; and re-enters the netting waterfall the next year.</p>

<p>Example: in 2026 you realize a $30,000 long-term loss with no offsetting gains. You deduct $3,000 against ordinary income this year. The remaining $27,000 carries to 2027 and beyond.</p>

<ul>
  <li>If 2027 brings a $20,000 long-term gain, the carryforward wipes it out. You also deduct another $3,000 against ordinary income, and $4,000 rolls to 2028.</li>
  <li>If 2027 brings only modest gains, you again deduct $3,000 &mdash; and the rest waits for a year with bigger gains.</li>
</ul>

<p>Investors who took big losses in a bad market year can shield gains for a decade or more. Track the carryforward carefully &mdash; it is reported in Part III of Schedule D each year, and a missed entry is a missed deduction. For background on how the underlying rates differ, see our piece on <a href="/blog/capital-gains-tax-short-term-vs-long-term">short-term vs long-term capital gains</a>.</p>

<h2>The wash sale rule: the booby trap</h2>

<p>The wash sale rule (<strong>IRS Section 1091</strong>) is the single biggest reason a harvested loss gets disallowed. The rule:</p>

<blockquote>If you sell a security at a loss and buy a "substantially identical" security within 30 days <em>before</em> or 30 days <em>after</em> the sale, the loss is disallowed for the current year.</blockquote>

<p>That is a 61-day window centered on the sale date. The disallowed loss is not gone forever &mdash; it is added to the cost basis of the replacement security, deferring (not eliminating) the deduction. But for the current year, your harvest produces zero tax benefit.</p>

<h3>What counts as "substantially identical"</h3>

<p>The IRS does not publish a formal list. In practice:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Same stock, same class</strong> &mdash; identical. Selling Apple and buying Apple is a wash sale.</li>
  <li><strong>Same mutual fund or ETF</strong> &mdash; identical.</li>
  <li><strong>Two ETFs that track the same index from different issuers</strong> (e.g., one S&amp;P 500 fund swapped for another) &mdash; the IRS has not formally ruled these "substantially identical," and they are the workhorse of wash-sale-safe harvesting. The conservative move is still to swap to a fund tracking a <em>different</em> index (e.g., total US market instead of S&amp;P 500).</li>
  <li><strong>Bonds with materially different issuer, coupon, or maturity</strong> &mdash; generally not identical.</li>
</ul>

<h3>Wash sales across accounts and family</h3>

<p>The trap most people miss: wash sales apply across <strong>all accounts you and your spouse control</strong> &mdash; including IRAs and 401(k)s. If you sell a fund in your taxable account at a loss and your spouse's IRA buys the same fund seven days later through an automatic dividend reinvestment, that is a wash sale and the taxable-account loss is disallowed. According to the <a href="https://www.schwab.com/learn/story/primer-on-wash-sales" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Schwab wash-sale primer</a>, the basis-adjustment relief is even harsher in the IRA case: the disallowed loss is permanently lost, because IRA basis is not tracked the same way taxable-account basis is.</p>

<p>Before harvesting, audit:</p>

<ul>
  <li>Your taxable brokerage's automatic dividend reinvestment (DRIP) settings.</li>
  <li>Any 401(k) target-date funds that hold the security.</li>
  <li>Your spouse's accounts.</li>
  <li>Recurring purchase plans (Acorns, M1, Robinhood recurring buys, payroll-deduction plans).</li>
</ul>

<h3>Crypto exception (for now)</h3>

<p>As of April 2026, cryptocurrency is <strong>not</strong> subject to the wash sale rule. The IRS classifies digital assets as property rather than securities, so Section 1091 does not reach them. You can sell Bitcoin at a loss and rebuy it the same day. Congress has proposed closing this loophole multiple times, so verify current law before relying on it for the next tax year.</p>

<h2>2026 brackets: who benefits most</h2>

<p>The 0% long-term capital-gains bracket is unusually generous in 2026. According to the <a href="https://taxfoundation.org/data/all/federal/2026-tax-brackets/" target="_blank" rel="noopener noreferrer">Tax Foundation's 2026 bracket summary</a>, single filers pay 0% on long-term gains up to $49,450 of taxable income; married couples filing jointly, up to $98,900. The 15% rate runs up to $545,500 single / $613,700 joint; above that you hit 20% (plus the 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax for higher earners).</p>

<p>This shifts who should harvest:</p>

<ul>
  <li><strong>0% bracket investors</strong> &mdash; usually should not harvest losses against long-term gains, because those gains were not going to be taxed anyway. Harvest only to take the $3,000 ordinary-income deduction.</li>
  <li><strong>15% bracket investors</strong> &mdash; harvesting losses against long-term gains saves 15 cents on every dollar of loss.</li>
  <li><strong>20% / NIIT-exposed investors</strong> &mdash; harvesting can save 23.8% (20% LTCG + 3.8% NIIT). Highest payoff per dollar of loss.</li>
  <li><strong>Anyone with short-term gains</strong> &mdash; losses that absorb short-term gains save at your full marginal ordinary rate (up to 37%). This is the most valuable use of a loss.</li>
</ul>

<h2>A 5-step harvesting playbook</h2>

<ol>
  <li><strong>Inventory unrealized losses by tax lot.</strong> In your brokerage, switch the cost-basis view to "specific identification" or "tax lot" view. You want to see each individual purchase, not just the average. A position that is flat overall may still contain individual lots with sizable losses.</li>
  <li><strong>Check year-to-date realized gains and losses.</strong> Compare against your tax projection. The most efficient harvest matches loss size to your realized short-term gains first, then long-term gains, then up to $3,000 against ordinary income.</li>
  <li><strong>Pick the replacement security first.</strong> Do not sell until you know what you are buying. Stay out of the market for 31+ days only if the price-risk does not bother you; otherwise buy a non-substantially-identical replacement on the same day.</li>
  <li><strong>Audit the 30-day window in every account.</strong> Pause DRIPs, recurring buys, and any 401(k) rebalancing that touches the same security. Repeat for your spouse's accounts.</li>
  <li><strong>Execute and document.</strong> Sell using "specific lot" so the brokerage realizes the highest-cost (largest loss) lots. Save the trade confirmations. After 31 days, you can swap back to the original holding if you wish.</li>
</ol>

<h2>When tax-loss harvesting does not help</h2>

<ul>
  <li><strong>You only have retirement-account losses.</strong> No taxable account, no harvest.</li>
  <li><strong>You are in the 0% LTCG bracket and have no short-term gains.</strong> Saving 0% &times; loss = $0.</li>
  <li><strong>The position has appreciated a lot since you bought it.</strong> You can only harvest unrealized losses. A $5,000 gain on the books cannot be harvested as a loss.</li>
  <li><strong>You are going to repurchase within 30 days anyway.</strong> The wash sale disallows the deduction.</li>
  <li><strong>Transaction costs eat the savings.</strong> Modern $0-commission brokers have largely removed this concern, but bid-ask spreads on thinly traded ETFs can still erode small harvests.</li>
  <li><strong>You will donate the appreciated position later.</strong> Donating appreciated stock to a qualified charity sidesteps capital-gains tax entirely and is often more tax-efficient than harvesting losses to offset the same gains.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Common mistakes</h2>

<ul>
  <li><strong>Forgetting reinvested dividends.</strong> A DRIP buy of $42 of a fund seven days after a tax-loss sale of the same fund will trigger a partial wash sale on a chunk of the loss.</li>
  <li><strong>Harvesting late in December and rebuying in early January.</strong> The 30-day window crosses tax years. Year-end harvests still need a 31-day gap.</li>
  <li><strong>Selling at a loss and gifting cash to your IRA the same week.</strong> If the IRA buys the same security, the loss is permanently disallowed &mdash; IRAs do not get the basis adjustment that taxable accounts do.</li>
  <li><strong>Ignoring state taxes.</strong> Most states tax capital gains at ordinary rates. Harvesting often saves both federal and state tax.</li>
  <li><strong>Trying to harvest a position you have held less than a year.</strong> Short-term and long-term losses are equally usable on Schedule D, but you may want to wait if doing so converts a future sale into a long-term gain. Run the math first.</li>
</ul>

<h2>Frequently Asked Questions</h2>

<h3>Is the $3,000 deduction limit per person or per return?</h3>
<p>Per return. A married couple filing jointly shares one $3,000 cap; married filing separately, $1,500 each. The cap has not changed since 1978 and is not indexed for inflation.</p>

<h3>How long can I carry a capital loss forward?</h3>
<p>Indefinitely. Unused capital losses carry over to subsequent tax years until exhausted, retaining their short-term or long-term character each year.</p>

<h3>Does the wash sale rule apply to ETFs?</h3>
<p>Yes. Two ETFs from the <em>same</em> issuer tracking the <em>same</em> index are typically considered substantially identical. ETFs from different issuers tracking different indexes (e.g., S&amp;P 500 vs total US market) are the standard wash-sale-safe replacement; swapping between two S&amp;P 500 funds from different issuers is a grey area the IRS has not formally ruled on.</p>

<h3>Can I harvest losses inside my Roth IRA or 401(k)?</h3>
<p>No. Losses inside tax-advantaged accounts produce no deduction, because those accounts are already shielded from capital-gains tax. Tax-loss harvesting is exclusively a taxable-account strategy.</p>

<h3>Does tax-loss harvesting work for cryptocurrency in 2026?</h3>
<p>Yes, and crypto enjoys an additional advantage: the IRS classifies digital assets as property rather than securities, so the wash sale rule does not currently apply. You can sell Bitcoin or Ether at a loss and immediately rebuy. Congress has proposed extending Section 1091 to crypto, so this exception may not last.</p>

<h3>If I have $20,000 of long-term gains and $25,000 of long-term losses, what happens?</h3>
<p>The $25,000 in losses fully offset the $20,000 in gains, leaving a $5,000 net loss. You deduct $3,000 against ordinary income this year and carry the remaining $2,000 forward as a long-term loss to the next tax year.</p>

<h3>Can I sell at a loss in December and buy back January 2 of the next year?</h3>
<p>No &mdash; that is still inside the 30-day window. The wash sale rule looks at calendar days, not tax years. A December 5 sale, for example, would require waiting until at least January 5 of the following year before repurchasing the same security.</p>
